Brainstorming is a structured, creative problem-solving technique designed to generate a high volume of diverse ideas by suspending judgment. Popularized in 1953 by advertising executive Alex Faickney Osborn in his book Applied Imagination, it helps individuals and cross-functional teams break out of conventional thinking patterns to discover unique, innovative solutions. [1, 2, 3] 
## The Core Rules of Brainstorming
To run an effective session and prevent groupthink or creative blocks, participants must adhere to four foundational principles: [4, 5] 

   1. Defer Judgment: Avoid criticizing, analyzing, or praising ideas during the generation phase. Evaluation happens much later.
   2. Prioritize Quantity: Focus on producing as many ideas as possible. A high volume naturally paves the way for higher quality later on.
   3. Encourage Wild Ideas: Welcome unusual, impractical, or seemingly impossible concepts. It is much easier to scale back a wild idea than it is to spice up a boring one.
   4. Build on Others: Combine, adapt, and expand on existing suggestions to create stronger, mutated concepts. [4, 5, 6] 

------------------------------
## Popular Brainstorming Techniques

| Technique | How it Works | Best Used For... |
|---|---|---|
| Brainwriting (6-3-5 Method) | 6 participants silently write down 3 ideas in 5 minutes, then pass their sheets to the next person to build upon. | Engaging introverts and avoiding conversational domination. |
| SCAMPER[](https://miro.com/brainstorming/what-is-brainstorming/) | Challenges teams to evaluate a problem through seven lenses: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, and Reverse. | Innovating on existing products, processes, or business services. |
| Rapid Ideation | Setting a strict, fast-paced time limit for participants to dump every single thought onto paper or digital stickies. | Overcoming initial overthinking and creative "cold starts". |
| Reverse Brainstorming | Instead of asking how to solve a problem, teams brainstorm how to cause or worsen the problem, then reverse those solutions. | Identifying hidden risks or systemic flaws in a plan. |

------------------------------
## The Process: From Chaos to Execution
A successful session moves fluidly from divergent thinking (expanding options) to convergent thinking (narrowing down to solutions): [7] 

* 
* Step 1: Frame the Problem: Clearly state the goal as an open-ended question (e.g., "In what ways might we improve onboarding?") and distribute background info ahead of time. [5, 8] 
* Step 2: Warm-Up: Spend a few minutes doing a light, unrelated creative drill to get everyone's mental gears turning. [6] 
* Step 3: Silent Ideation: Let individuals sketch or write thoughts independently for a brief window before sharing aloud to minimize early bias. [2, 9] 
* Step 4: Group Collection & Expansion: Facilitators track all incoming ideas visibly on collaborative tools like Miro, [Mural](https://www.mural.co/blog/brainstorming), or [Figma](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/what-is-brainstorming/). [2, 6, 10] 
* Step 5: Convergence: Sort ideas into strategic patterns using affinity mapping or decision matrices, vote on the strongest candidates, and assign explicit owners to next steps. [2, 11]
